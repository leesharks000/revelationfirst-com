The anchor is one sentence, and its subject is unexpressed

Irenaeus, writing around 180 CE, says that ‘it’ or ‘he’ was seen not long ago, near the end of Domitian’s reign. The subject of ἐωράθη is unexpressed. Grammatically the verb can refer either to the apocalypse or to the seer, although most modern interpreters judge the vision-reading contextually preferable.

εἰ γὰρ ἔδει ἀναφανδὸν τῷ νῦν καιρῷ κηρύττεσθαι τοὔνομα αὐτοῦ, δι᾽ ἐκείνου ἂν ἐρρήθη τοῦ καὶ τὴν ἀποκάλυψιν ἑωρακότος·For if it were necessary that his name be proclaimed openly at the present time, it would have been spoken through the one who saw the apocalypse; for it was seen not long ago…Irenaeus, Adv. Haer. 5.30.3

The proper conclusion is not that the late date collapses, but that the external evidence supplies a strong reception memory rather than an inscriptional date.

A consensus that reversed

In the late nineteenth century, major critical scholars placed the Apocalypse under Nero or Galba. Philip Schaff reassigned it to 68 or 69 ‘with the majority of modern critics’. Lightfoot, Westcott, Hort, Baur and Renan disagreed on much else while finding a pre-70 Apocalypse plausible. The early date was not inherently a preterist or conservative programme; it belonged to the critical mainstream of its moment. Milligan, himself a defender of the later date, conceded in 1893 that recent scholarship had decided, with little exception, for the earlier date.

The twentieth century reversed that distribution. Swete, Charles and later commentaries re-established the Domitianic date, which by the later twentieth century functioned as the default in introductions. The reversal did not occur because a newly discovered first-century object dated the Apocalypse. It occurred through changed evaluations of Irenaeus, the social setting of the Asian churches, imperial cult evidence, and linguistic development.

Those are legitimate historical reasons. The historiographic point is narrower: the same broad dossier has supported opposite critical majorities, and recent work continues to produce both early and late reconstructions.

What follows, and what does not

Early is not yet first. This must be said before anything else, because it is the easiest place for the argument to overreach. The strongest internal arguments — the standing temple, the imperial count, the Nero cipher — commonly yield dates in the 60s. Van Kooten locates the book in 68–69. Those dates challenge the Domitianic consensus. They do not by themselves precede 1 Thessalonians on the conventional chronology. That is rung II.

Defeat condition. An external or material anchor fixing the received form of Revelation after 70 CE. Reception memory, however early and however broad, is not such an anchor.
