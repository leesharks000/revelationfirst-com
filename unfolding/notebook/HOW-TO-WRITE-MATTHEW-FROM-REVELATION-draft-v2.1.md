## 0. The question

0.1. The received account, in full. Matthew is a life of Jesus written in Greek late in the first century by an author who had Mark before him and reworked it. What Matthew has beyond Mark came from a sayings collection shared with Luke, or from Luke's use of Matthew, and from material of Matthew's own, written or oral, shaped by scripture. Revelation is another kind of book, a prophecy sent to seven assemblies in Asia, and where the two agree in wording they draw on a common stock: the Septuagint, the sayings of Jesus in circulation, the diction of early Christian prophecy. On this account nothing passes from Revelation to Matthew. Where anything passes between them it passes the other way, from the tradition of the sayings into the prophet's book.

0.2. This paper asks a different question and does not begin by disputing that account. Suppose a writer has Revelation, whole, and wants a Gospel. What must that writer do? Which operations, applied to which places, give which parts of Matthew? The question is answered by doing it. Each section takes a place or several places of Revelation, states an operation, performs it, and sets the result beside Matthew's Greek.

0.3. Three things are meant by "write" and they are kept apart. (a) A relation in Revelation and a relation in Matthew are the same relation with a stated change: this is shown by quotation and by counts over the whole New Testament. (b) The change is one of a small set of moves that recur: this is shown by the ledger. (c) A move, given to a reader who is shown only the place in Revelation and the rule, produces Matthew's passage: this has been done once, under seal, and is reported in section VII. Most of what follows is (a) and (b). Only section VII is (c). A fourth kind of support appears from section II on: a line written down before it was counted. Where a pairing was so written, the text says so, and section XIV gives the tally with the failures.

0.4. What the demonstration shows, and what it does not. It shows a procedure that another reader can run, alter, reverse or break, and it measures how much of Matthew the procedure reaches. It does not, taken alone, say who wrote first: every pairing below can be read from right to left, as the work of a seer who had Matthew and compressed it. The paper keeps to that limit in every section that demonstrates. It does not keep silent about the larger argument. A count of how much of one book can be written from another has no weight until it is said why the count matters, and section P says it: what the programme holds, on what grounds held elsewhere in the archive, and what it expects to show. The two registers are marked wherever they meet. *Shown here* means shown in this paper with its data. *Held* means the programme's position, argued in the records named.

0.5. The source is held open (Figure 2, at XIII.5). A writer who knows a book does not move through it in order. Any place may be drawn on at any point of the new work, several at once, and the same place twice. Tests that looked for Revelation's sequence in Matthew's sequence did not find it, by any of the tests run (section XIV; the notebook, 2BB, 2BD, 2BE). The unit is the relation: who does what to whom, in what mode.

0.6. Matthew treats Revelation like scripture. This is the paper's account of what the procedure is, and it is stated here because everything after reads differently under it. Matthew's handling of the Septuagint is known and uncontested: a phrase kept, a saying of a prophet staged as an event, a scene restaged with another bearer (Pharaoh and the children, Herod and the children), two scriptures run together in one line. The operations of sections I to XI are these operations, with Revelation in the place of the prophet. Counted on the same instrument, in the notebook (2BG), the likeness holds at three points and fails at two.

- Order. Matthew's thirteen uses of Isaiah stand in no order of Isaiah (rank correlation −0.14), its fourteen of the Psalms in no order of the Psalter (+0.01). Revelation's figure is −0.09. No one supposes Matthew walked Isaiah from the first chapter to the last, and the test for a walked source was the wrong test.
- Company. Of the verses where Matthew uses scripture without naming it, 55 per cent are also reached from Revelation, against 28.6 per cent of the Gospel at large. Of the verses where Matthew cites scripture with a formula, 22 per cent. Revelation is worked in where scripture is worked in tacitly, in the same verses.
- The one walk. Revelation 12:1–6 is taken in order through Matthew 2, beside the infancy of Moses in the same chapter: narrative scripture walked for narrative. The order then named a verse before the verse was read. ὁ δράκων ἕστηκεν ἐνώπιον τῆς γυναικὸς τῆς μελλούσης τεκεῖν; ἐστάθη ἐπάνω οὗ ἦν τὸ παιδίον, of the star. A star and a standing in one verse are found in those two verses of the New Testament and no other.
- Against: fewer of Revelation's words are kept than scripture's (a median of one shared stem to a row, against two for the allusions commonly granted). What happens in the source is often reversed; what the source judges of it is reversed twice in 158 rows, and one of the two stands inside Matthew's open reversal of the law (S.6).
- And it is never named. The book presents itself as the speech of Jesus (1:1; the seven letters). In a telling of his life that speech is spoken by him. A formula of citation has no place to stand.

Section S does this on the page, operation by operation, scripture beside Revelation. The scripture lists in the counts are from memory, checked by machine against the seated Septuagint, and the counts are first ones (S.7). If it stands, the rows where the Septuagint also reaches a verse stop being cases against Revelation and become what Matthew does with two scriptures at once.

0.7. What changed from the first version (v1.0, 27 November 2026). Sections 0 and P were rewritten and S is new. The ledger grew from 86 rows to 158, and the new regions stand in their steps: the opening of Revelation in Matthew 11 (I.5a), the walk through the infancy (II.6a), the discourse to the assembly (V.6a), the kings of the earth and the ransom (VI.6c), Herodias (VIII.5a), the door (IX.4a), the followers on the night (X.5a). XIII.1c says how a word changes between the books and what a row entered for relation must pass. XIV carries the new counts and the new failures, the largest being that no order was found. One sentence of v1.0 is reversed: P.6 there called the burden between an extant book and a supposed document symmetrical; it lies on the supposed one (P.3, P.9).

0.8. What changed from the first reissue (v1.1). The source is no longer one book. Three bodies of extant text are laid beside Revelation and read against Matthew by the ledger's way: the letters (section E), Josephus (section J), and, as a second book read against the same source, the Gospel of Thomas (section T). Section M sets the resulting map beside the derivation and keeps the two apart. P.2a adds the argument from the order of forms. XIII.1d proposes a fourth parameter, takes the relation one level below the word, and adds a rule of composition. XIV.8 gives what failed, the tallies, and three corrections of method. Sections I to XII stand as deposited, with a pointer where a new section bears on them.

0.9. Three rules that now bind every section, each at the author's word (XIV.8). No received date is a premise, and the order of the Gospels is a premise in neither direction. A control is a text outside the hypothesized system. No hypothetical source is supposed for any verse: what no extant text reaches is written "unexplained". And one distinction: a derivation (a relation with its move, counted, and at one place run blind) and a map (a named pair whose words stand in both texts) are two measures, 28.6 per cent and 61.8 to 64.9 per cent, and the distance between them is the work that remains.

## P. The programme this belongs to

P.1. Revelation First is the thesis that the Apocalypse is the generative source of the rest of the New Testament: that the epistles and then the Gospels were unfolded from it. It is distinct from the argument that Revelation was written early. The early date is old (Robinson 1976; Gentry 1989) and says nothing about what came from what. That the whole corpus unfolds from the Apocalypse has, to the author's knowledge, no antecedent as a sustained argument; Newton in 1733 held that Hebrews and the Petrine letters allude to it, and the Muratorian fragment has Paul following John (#1735 §I). The thesis can be held at three strengths: that Revelation can generate the later books by stated procedures; that the New Testament reads as one corpus with Revelation at its head; that Revelation was written first. The author holds the third, on the evidence, and grants that a reader may accept the first alone. This paper demonstrates only at the first. The rest of this section is the argument inside which the demonstration has its weight.

P.2. The literary-genetic argument (held; argued in "Revelation First: Literary Genealogy and the Missing Ancestors of the Epistles", #1735). A literature proposed as earliest should be tested as an ancestor and not only dated as an object. Its forms should be derivable from materials that exist and were available before it; and what follows it may be expected to show transformations of it. For any feature of a text the ancestry on offer is one of four kinds: an extant antecedent; a tradition attested independently; an antecedent reconstructed because the later text seems to need one; an innovation left unexplained.

Put to Revelation, the test returns extant antecedents throughout. The throne and the living creatures are Ezekiel's, the beasts and the one like a son of man Daniel's, the lampstand Zechariah's, the plagues those of Exodus, the city Isaiah's and Ezekiel's; the seven and the Logos in their midst are in Philo, word for word enough that the founders of the modern commentary printed the passage beside the lampstands (#1758; Bousset 1906; Charles 1920). The genealogy of the book can be traced, inspected and mapped down to the letter, and no Christian document has to be supposed before it to explain any part of it.

Put to the epistles and the Gospels in the received order, the test returns reconstructions. Paul's letters arrive with a Christian grammar already made: formulae marked as received (1 Cor 15:3; 11:23), a hymn whose earlier life has a literature of its own (Phil 2:6–11), and in the first letter on the received chronology a "word of the Lord" (1 Thess 4:15) that no one can produce. The field's own category, pre-Pauline, names the gap. For the Gospels the list is longer: a sayings source, a special source for Matthew and one for Luke, a passion narrative before Mark, a small apocalypse beneath Mark 13, a signs source beneath John, collections of testimonies, and behind them a tradition carried by mouth. None is extant. Each is inferred from the texts it is brought in to explain.

So the asymmetry. The one book of the New Testament whose literary ancestry is complete in surviving texts is placed last and read as derivative. The books whose ancestry has to be supplied by documents no one has seen are placed first and read as sources. Put Revelation first and the direction of explanation runs through extant texts at each step: the Septuagint and Philo into the Apocalypse (shown, in the records named), the Apocalypse into the epistles (shown in part, P.5), the Apocalypse with scripture into the Gospels (shown in part, here). In the reverse order the same steps cannot be taken without intermediaries made up for the purpose. The programme holds a further member of the first step, the Slavonic Josephus, argued in "The Word That Became Text" and not relied on here.

P.2a. The order of forms (the author's argument, with one count). The received sequence, in full: sayings and stories of a teacher passed on by mouth; Paul's letters, with little of the life in them; then Mark, Matthew and Luke with a sayings source, and John; then Revelation, a late and separate work. The same account holds that the movement was apocalyptic from its first day. The author: "an intra nt narrative of beginning with nauve, crufty biography and evolving towards the abstract and visionary simply contradicts *everything* we know about second temple literature." And: "the received account puts *two separate* unexplained genre innovations first, separately. tbe epistolary form; and then as a formally unrelated later innovation, biographical tradition with no surviving text. two. not one. both of which require their own imaginary texts to explain."

Put as a burden. The letter of an apostle to an assembly, read as scripture, is a new form. The told life of a teacher of one's own time is a new form. The vision is the period's old and working form (Daniel, the books of Enoch, and after them Ezra and Baruch; stated from general knowledge of the period's literature and not counted here). An account of these books owes an explanation of the two new forms. The received order puts the two inventions first, apart, each resting on its own shelf of texts that do not survive (P.2), and the inherited form last. The author's order derives both from a book that exists: the letter from the book that opens and closes as one and holds seven, and the life from that book and the letters together (section E).

The count. χάρις ὑμῖν καὶ εἰρήνη ἀπὸ: grace, peace and "from" in one verse stand in thirteen verses of the New Testament, the greeting of twelve letters under Paul's name and Revelation 1:4. A book whose last verse is "grace … with" you or all: thirteen books, eleven under Paul's name, Hebrews, and Revelation (22:21). So the new form's frame stands whole, opening and close, on the one book in the old form, addressed to seven assemblies. The count cannot say which way the greeting travelled. The argument ranks two orders by what each has to assume, and shows nothing about any one verse.

P.2b. Successive innovation (proposed by a reviewer of draft v2.0; taken up as a line of test, with no order assumed). The genealogy of P.2 can be read as a chain of documents sharing material. It can also be read as a succession of capacities, each station able to do something the one before could not.

| Station | What the figure can do there that it could not before |
|---|---|
| The Septuagint | figures, events and offices of scripture |
| Philo | those offices ordered as philosophy: the Logos in the midst of the seven, the cutter, the image |
| Revelation | the office is a named presence that speaks in the first person, promises, reproves and commissions writing |
| The letters | the figure has assemblies, named followers and a conduct owed to it |
| Josephus | circumstance: a land's rulers, sects, coins and incidents, and the practice of writing a life in Greek |
| Matthew | the figure has a narrated past |

This is a genealogy of capacities and no chronology. It is offered so that direction can be asked a second way, apart from which words are kept: which innovations presuppose which others? One worked case shows the question and its difficulty. Matthew 16:18 needs two things to exist before it can be written: apostles as the foundation of a building (Rev 21:14; Eph 2:20) and a follower named Cephas who is called a pillar (Gal 2:9). The relation in Matthew exists only in their fusion (E.4). But the received account reads the same three texts the other way, with Revelation's twelve names presupposing a told life in which twelve were chosen. Presupposition can be argued in both directions until there is a rule for it, fixed before the cases are read. That rule is owed. The reviewer adds a station for Thomas, "the figure's speech becomes an interpretive encounter"; the author has not ruled where Thomas stands, and section T places it nowhere in an order.

P.3. How Revelation was kept from consideration as a source (held; documented in "I Know You Are, But What Am I?", #1746, and in "Secondary Source", #1741). This is described as an arrangement and its effects. No motive is claimed for it and none is needed.

In a source argument a text has standing when it can be presupposed without first being proved, when material can be assigned to it, when a text of it can be established, and when it is admitted as a candidate wherever a source is sought. In eighteen works of source criticism from Lachmann (1835) to Streeter (1924), read in the original languages at the locus, the reconstructed documents hold all four powers and the Apocalypse holds none. Holtzmann sets aside in advance any inference that presupposes a relation between the Apocalypse and the Gospels, and proceeds from sources his own inquiry constructs. Streeter assumes the sayings source and dates the extant book last. At one verse the record runs the other way: in 1843 Hitzig argued from Matthew 24:30 and Revelation 1:7 that Matthew used the Apocalypse, and called it a compelling proof; over sixty years the dependence was granted, as possible or as fact, by Volkmar, Keim, Bernhard Weiss, Bousset and Merx. Each acknowledgment stands where it leaves the source theories untouched: a footnote to a date, an evangelist's insertion, a note in a commentary on the other book. A census run before Hitzig was read returned his pairing: the tribes, the mourning and the clouds stand together in those two verses and in no other window of some 2.5 million words of Greek (#1746 §IV). The pairing is a row of this paper's ledger, produced by the engine.

The reception did the rest by parts (#1741). The book was received as derivative in its author (a second John), its date (the end of Domitian), its matter (sources, myth, a letter of Paul), its figure (the Gospels' Jesus, already known) and its language (charged to the writer as bad Greek). The relations that run out of it, the Muratorian fragment's report and the crown of life it shares with James, were answered or left unused. The generative position was given to the Gospels, to Paul, and to documents reconstructed behind them.

The effect on this paper's question is exact. The reason no one has counted how much of Matthew can be written from Revelation is that the question had no place to be asked: the book was dated out of candidacy before any source was sought, and the place a source would fill was already held by a hypothesis. An extant text does not need to prove that it exists. A hypothetical one does (#1746). The burden has been carried the other way round.

P.4. Why the count matters. On the received account nothing passes from Revelation to Matthew, and what the two share is common stock. That account predicts that a procedure such as this one reaches little, and that what it reaches is scattered, weak, and explained as well by the Septuagint.

*Shown here.* The ledger has 158 relations reaching 305 of Matthew's 1,067 verses, 28.6 per cent; 265 verses without the rows entered for relation alone (XIV.1). By the kind of material, on the paper's proxy for the strata (XIV.1a): 100 of the 458 verses Matthew has from Mark; 70 of the 184 of the double tradition; 135 of the 425 that are Matthew's own. The two strata for which lost documents are posited, the double tradition and Matthew's own material, are the two the procedure reaches furthest into: 38 and 32 per cent. This was reached in two days (the first version of this paper, a day earlier, had 86 relations and 178 verses), by one author working with a machine, from a tagged text, with every row checked in both books and the lines that were written down before counting tallied with their failures (XIV). It was not dug for. Whole regions were opened by a single reading: Matthew 18 from the seven letters, the night of the arrest as the letters' promises staged and failed, Matthew 11 as the opening of the Apocalypse. The last was the author's reading of chapters held out from the search, registered before any count, and run once: five of ten lines held, among them σὺ εἶ ὁ ἐρχόμενος, John's question, against the greeting John sends from ὁ ὢν καὶ ὁ ἦν καὶ ὁ ἐρχόμενος, and ᾧ ἐὰν βούληται ὁ υἱὸς ἀποκαλύψαι against the title of the book, Ἀποκάλυψις Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, ἣν ἔδωκεν αὐτῷ ὁ θεός.

*Since the first reissue.* The ledger is unchanged at 158 rows and 305 verses. Beside it now stands a map of named pairs between Matthew and the letters, Josephus and the Septuagint, which with the ledger reaches 61.8 to 64.9 per cent of the Gospel (section M). The two are different measures and 28.6 per cent remains the figure of record.

What the figure is and is not. A row is a relation with a stated change, and most rows share one or two words; it is not verbatim agreement, and 28.6 per cent is a reach, the share of verses some row touches. The control that would turn a reach into a rate is owed: the same procedure run from another book into Matthew, and from Revelation into a book that is no Gospel (XIV). Until it is run the paper claims the reach and the sealed tallies, and no more.

Why the reach matters even so. The sayings source is a hypothesis to explain some 184 verses of this Gospel. A book that exists, by a procedure that is written out and can be rerun, touches 305, and seventy of them are in the sayings source's own ground. Whatever the control returns, that is a candidate. A field that admits a lost document on the strength of what it would explain has, on its own practice, to admit an extant one and count it.

P.5. The epistles are the easier case, for three reasons that are on the surface. The book contains seven letters, so the form is there to be expanded. Ancient testimony says so in as many words: the Muratorian fragment has Paul writing to seven assemblies by name *sequens prodecessoris sui Johannis ordinem*, following the order of his predecessor John (seated: Tregelles 1867). And there is the matter of ancestry. Letters are everywhere in the Jewish and Greek worlds, and the claim is not about letters as such. It is about one form: a letter from one who speaks for the risen figure to an assembly, with address, commendation, correction and promise. For that form the one surviving ancestor between the Septuagint and Paul is this (#1735). The figure in the midst does not send messages to the assemblies. He gives them to the messengers and commissions them to go and give them: the book commissions future writing, and the epistles are what it commissions.

P.6. What is provisional or has failed for the Gospels. The assignment of the four living creatures and the four riders to the four Gospels came out within chance when counted, and agrees with Irenaeus only under a rule made after the fact. No order of Revelation was found in John, and none in Matthew taken whole, by strand, or by letter and chapter, save the one walk of 0.6. No single law per Gospel was found. Luke, Mark and John have no procedure yet. Matthew has this one, and it is a working draft.

P.7. The Gospels are the harder case, and this paper takes the harder case. A letter unfolded from a letter keeps its form. A Gospel unfolded from an apocalypse changes everything a reader sees first: vision becomes narrative, the first person becomes the third, heaven becomes a land with towns in it, and what is about to come becomes what happened. No ancient witness says a Gospel was written so. And a Gospel has a second source that cannot be removed from any account of it. If a stated procedure nevertheless reaches from Revelation into Matthew's own material, in Greek, with counts, that is evidence of a different weight from the epistolary case.

P.8. What the programme holds that it will show. These are claims, made in the open so that they can fail. None is shown in this paper.

1. The procedure, extended by the same means, will reach the greater part of what Matthew has beyond Mark, and a large part of what it has from Mark.
2. Mark, Luke and John will each yield a procedure of their own from the same book, and the differences among the four Gospels will be differences of procedure.
3. Of what the documents posited behind the Gospels are brought in to explain, the remainder when Revelation, the Septuagint, Philo, the letters and Josephus have been read against it will be small. No hypothetical document is supposed for it at any stage: it is held open as unexplained, and a fusion can make material of its own (M.7).
4. The figure of Jesus can be followed innovation by innovation through texts that exist, from the Septuagint and Philo to the Apocalypse and from the Apocalypse to the epistles and the Gospels, with no intermediary supplied.
5. The New Testament reads as one corpus made from one book, and Revelation was written first.
6. The hand. The author's hypothesis, stated in the author's words and shown nowhere in this paper: "the entire movement is josephus bipgraphicalizing philo through the visionary revelation", and "josephus may be the missing context" (section J).

A reader may hold the paper to what it shows and the programme to what it claims. The paper asks that the two not be exchanged: that its 28.6 per cent not be reported as a proof of the fifth claim, and that the fifth claim not be used to dismiss the 28.6 per cent.

P.9. What follows if it holds. First, the materials for which the study of the Gospels posits lost documents are here set against a book that exists and can be inspected, doing some of the same work, and the burden lies on the document that has to be supposed. Second, if the figure of the Gospels can be produced from the figure of the Apocalypse, then that figure is a work of literature before it is a biography, and the search for the person behind the Gospels has a text in front of it that it has not been reading as a source. Third, if Matthew handles the Apocalypse as scripture, the Apocalypse was scripture to the writer of the first Gospel: the latest book of the canon, on the received dating, was among the writings a Gospel was made from. Fourth, the book that the canon puts last and the commentaries treat as derivative would stand at the head.

P.10. Why this will be resisted, stated as costs and not as motives. Items (a) to (c) describe how the field is arranged and can be checked against its handbooks. Items (d) and (e) are predictions of reception and are offered as predictions. (a) Every standing solution to the Synoptic problem is a relation among three Gospels and one or two hypothetical documents; none has a place for an extant fourth source of another genre, and admitting one reopens each. (b) The received chronology, with Revelation near 95 and Paul near 50, is built into every introduction, commentary and syllabus; a result that needs another order has nowhere to be filed. (c) Apocalypse and Gospel belong to different specialisms with different journals; work across the line is reviewed by neither. (d) Historical study of Jesus has a person as its object, and confession has a person as its Lord; a book in that place removes the one and renames the other. (e) A reader, human or machine, formed on the field's literature will answer that this "needs to be shown." It does, and the answer is given here in the only way available: by showing it where it can be shown, saying where it cannot, and publishing the data and scripts so that the reader can run them. The same reader does not ask that the sayings source be shown.

## S. Matthew and scripture: the mode of composition

S.1. The received account, in full. Matthew is the Gospel most given to scripture. Some ten times it stops the story to say that a thing happened so that what was spoken through a prophet might be fulfilled, and gives the words. Far more often it uses scripture without saying so: a psalm's line told as an event, a prophet's figure met on the road, an old story told again of a new person. The handbooks sort this into quotation, allusion and typology, and none of it is in dispute. What follows takes five operations Matthew performs on the Septuagint, each at a place where no one doubts it, and sets beside each the same operation performed on Revelation. The scripture cases are the calibration. The Revelation cases are rows of the ledger.

The count cannot tell a conflation from two writers each going to Daniel. The rows above can, where Revelation holds what the scripture does not.

S.8. The nine steps and twenty-one rules are a description, from outside, of a writer composing from a scripture: the book lies open, any place may be drawn on, a saying is given circumstances so that it can happen, and the circumstances come from the land's names, from the Septuagint and from the book again. *Not shown:* the direction. A seer could have gone to Matthew. What that seer would then have done is another set of operations (a denial written as an assembly's praise, a guiding star written as stars thrown down), and whether those are operations this writer performs on any source is a question the notebook has asked and not settled (2BH; 2BI.3).

I.6. The other accounts of the same thing. Three positions have been taken on the sayings the exalted speaker utters in Revelation. This paper is a fourth, and it is nearer the oldest of the three than the newest.

(a) Bultmann. Form criticism held that prophets, speaking in the first person as the risen Lord, supplied sayings which the tradition then carried as sayings of Jesus, and it pointed to Revelation as the place where the process could be watched, 16:15 among its examples (*History of the Synoptic Tradition*, ET 1963, 127, as Bauckham reports it in his opening notes). Bauckham's article opens from this: the words of the exalted Christ in the Apocalypse are the one undisputed first-century case of utterance in Christ's name in the first person, and so the standing exhibit of everyone who holds that prophets made sayings. The direction worked in this section, from the speaker's saying to the teacher's, was a mainstream position of the last century for sayings of exactly this kind. What that position never proposed is that the exhibit is the source. It reasoned from Revelation to lost oracles like Revelation's. Here the extant book does the work the lost oracles were asked to do.

(b) Vos (1965), the one systematic study, and not read: no open copy was found (see Works cited). As reported by those who have it: twenty-five sayings of Jesus found in Revelation, in a form that holds steady under variation; direct use distinguished from indirect; the conclusion that the seer knew a fixed tradition of the sayings, carried orally (Tripaldi, 6–7, with notes 23–31). Bauckham, in the same notes, calls that conclusion something of an overreaction to (a) and observes that Vos scarcely takes up the question of the prophets. One item bears on the programme directly. At Revelation 2:10b, the crown of life, Vos is reported to find a probable unwritten saying of Jesus (Vos 192–193, as Tripaldi cites it, n. 31). That is the place from which the programme derives James (P.3). On the report, the received account has no source for the promise there either, and supplies one that is not extant.

(c) Bauckham (1977; again 1983; reprinted 1993, 92–117). Read at its first page, in the passages of the reprint that are publicly excerpted, and through a review; not read in full. The thief of 3:3 and 16:15 and the knocking of 3:20 are taken to depend on the Synoptic parables of the Thief and the Watching Servants. Its first three numbered conclusions: such parables were in wide use from the beginning, and for these two the evidence outside the Gospels is good (1 Thessalonians 5:2; 2 Peter 3:10; Didache 16:1; and the versions in Thomas 21 and 103); they were collected and paired early; and use in exhortation wore the story away and left the saying: the word is deparabolization, carried furthest at 3:20, where the figure of the story says "I".

The observation in (c) is the same as I.2. The operation is the inverse. So there is agreement on the textual relation and disagreement on the operation that produced it, and the disagreement is older than this paper: (a) and (c) already hold the two directions between them. One datum both owe an account of. Outside the Gospels and Thomas the thief is everywhere a saying and nowhere a story: 1 Thessalonians 5:2, 2 Peter 3:10, Revelation 3:3 and 16:15. On (c) that is one story worn away four times. On the account here it is one saying kept four times and given a householder once. The count does not decide between them.

Section VII returns to this, because there a story is shown arising from a saying under a stated rule. The inverse can be built the same way: a rule that takes the parable and yields the saying, written down, run under seal, with cuts. It has not been built, and it is invited. Its test would be whether what it yields accounts for where these sayings stand in Revelation: in the letters, each fitted to its assembly.

I.7. Rule M1. A saying of the speaker in the first person, a promise, a warning or a formula, is kept as a saying of the teacher. Twenty rows of the ledger keep.

V.3a. A second source at the same place. Revelation gives the oath by heaven and nothing of what Matthew puts in its stead. Philo does. On the third commandment: ὁ γὰρ τοῦ σπουδαίου, φησί, λόγος ὅρκος ἔστω, let the word of the good be an oath; and if one must add something, not the highest cause at once, ἀλλὰ γῆν, ἥλιον, ἀστέρας, οὐρανόν (De specialibus legibus 2.2 and 2.5). And: κάλλιστον δὴ καὶ βιωφελέστατον καὶ ἁρμόττον λογικῇ φύσει τὸ ἀνώμοτον (De decalogo 84). Matthew: ἔστω δὲ ὁ λόγος ὑμῶν ναὶ ναί, οὒ οὔ, and no oath by heaven, none by earth. "Word" as the subject of "let it be", in a passage on oaths: in Philo here, and again in a fragment under Philo's name that the edition prints in its apparatus at De decalogo 83 (ἔστω οὖν ὁ λόγος ἰσότιμος ὅρκῳ); never in Josephus, never in the Septuagint, and in the New Testament at Matthew 5:37 only. So Matthew keeps Philo's sentence and opposes Philo's allowance, by the two moves already in the table, at the place where the row from Revelation had left the positive half unsupplied. This was looked for on the author's word that Philo might close gaps, and it was named before the search. It is one place, and it was found by running the wrong edge, from Philo straight to Matthew. Run the right way it holds better. Philo has two tiers: the highest cause, by whom one should be slow to swear, and earth, sun, stars and heaven, by which one may. And of God's own oath: λόγοι εἰσὶν ὅρκοι βεβαιούμενοι ἔργων ἀποτελέσμασι, his words are oaths (Legum allegoriae 3.203–206). Revelation's angel swears on the upper tier and names the lower as that one's works: by the living one who made heaven and earth and sea. Matthew then forbids both tiers and keeps the sentence about the word. Philo, Revelation, Matthew, each working on the one before.

V.4. The petition. κἀγώ σε τηρήσω ἐκ τῆς ὥρας τοῦ πειρασμοῦ. In the prayer: μὴ εἰσενέγκῃς ἡμᾶς εἰς πειρασμόν, ἀλλὰ ῥῦσαι ἡμᾶς ἀπὸ τοῦ πονηροῦ. What the speaker promises an assembly, the followers are taught to ask. The speaker is exchanged and the sentence turns from promise to petition. Luke 11:4 has the first half.

The holy, the dogs, the pearls, entering, the gate: five members inside eight verses of Matthew. V.5a. What changes here is the mode. In Revelation these are parts of a place: its gates, what they are made of, who is inside, who is outside. In Matthew they are things to do and not to do. The city becomes an ethics. It also accounts for a sentence that has always sat oddly in the Sermon: why the holy, dogs and pearls should come together in one saying. In the city they are together already.

V.5b. Looked for further, at a reviewer's suggestion, with each line written down before counting. One more held: ὄψονται τὸ πρόσωπον αὐτοῦ and ὅτι αὐτοὶ τὸν Θεὸν ὄψονται. "They shall see", with God or the face as what is seen, stands at these two verses only. What the city's people do, the pure in heart are promised. One is weaker: mourning ended (οὔτε πένθος; μακάριοι οἱ πενθοῦντες, ὅτι αὐτοὶ παρακληθήσονται), where Isaiah 61:2 is nearer, παρακαλέσαι πάντας τοὺς πενθοῦντας. Two did not hold. The excluded of the city (murderers, fornicators, liars: 21:8; 22:15) against the first antitheses (murder, adultery, oaths): the same three stand together in five other places (Matthew 15 and 19, Mark 10, Luke 18, 1 Timothy 1) and the commandments are behind them all. And the Sermon does not share more of the vocabulary of Revelation 21–22 than Matthew 10, 23 or 24–25 do; it shares less. So the relation of the city to the Sermon is at particular places, the blessings and the close, and is not a general colouring.

V.5c. Philo had already made the city an ethics. This was written down before Philo was opened, and found as written and more. On the psalm of the river that gladdens the city of God, Philo asks which city, since the present holy city, with its temple, lies far from sea and rivers; and answers: ἡ δὲ θεοῦ πόλις ὑπὸ Ἑβραίων Ἱερουσαλὴμ καλεῖται, ἧς μεταληφθὲν τοὔνομα ὅρασίς ἐστιν εἰρήνης. Do not look for it in regions of the earth, οὐ γὰρ ἐκ ξύλων ἢ λίθων δεδημιούργηται, but in a soul without war and sharp of sight. And then: μηδὲν οὖν διαφερέτω σοι ἢ ὅρασιν εἰρήνης ἢ ὅρασιν θεοῦ τὸ αὐτὸ ὑποκείμενον ὀνομάζειν (De somniis 2.246–254). Vision of peace and vision of God are one thing under two names, and the thing is the city.

Matthew's two blessings stand side by side, seeing God and then making peace, and they are the two halves of the name of the city that Revelation ends on. Three texts, and each has what the next needs: Philo reads the city's name and moves the city into a person; Revelation builds the city, with a river and without a temple, and has its people see the face; Matthew says the name's two halves as blessings and tells the hearers that they are the city. The move that V.5a called Matthew's is in Philo, stated, before either.

V.5d. The three do different work, and the difference should be kept. Philo's is an identity: two glosses of one name, said to mean one thing. Matthew's are two acts with two rewards, seeing and making peace, and "making" is Matthew's; Philo's city has peace and does not make it. Revelation has the seeing and has no word of peace in its city. So Matthew keeps from Revelation what Revelation built (the gates of pearl, the dogs outside, the entering, the seeing of the face) and has from Philo what Revelation's words do not carry (peace beside the seeing; the city as persons). Both at once, in one sermon. That is the pattern the third parameter names (XIII.1b), and this is its fullest case.

VI.1. Revelation has a relation of buying and selling with four bearers: the speaker, the Lamb, a rider, the adversary. Matthew tells all four, each turned its own way. Each construction below was searched through every verse of the New Testament; the lists are complete.

VI.6. One stretch of Matthew, 19:16–20:28, now has four places of the source in it: the rich one who must trade (Revelation 3:17–18); the seat given, δώσω αὐτῷ καθίσαι μετ’ ἐμοῦ ἐν τῷ θρόνῳ μου, kept at ἐπὶ δώδεκα θρόνους and turned at τὸ δὲ καθίσαι ἐκ δεξιῶν μου καὶ ἐξ εὐωνύμων οὐκ ἔστιν ἐμὸν τοῦτο δοῦναι, the only other place where "give" governs "to sit"; the rider's coin; and, between them, the story of the next section. VI.6a. Philo behind the rich one who is blind. The letter to Laodicea joins wealth and blindness and offers a wealth to be bought with sight restored: πτωχὸς καὶ τυφλὸς, and eye-salve ἵνα βλέπῃς. That pairing is Philo's standing figure: οὐ τυφλὸν πλοῦτον, βλέποντα δὲ καὶ σφόδρα ὀξυδορκοῦντα (De agricultura 54); πλοῦτος οὐχ ὁ τυφλός, the wealth that sees and takes no false coin (De fuga 19). Wealth with blindness in one section: seventeen times in Philo, never in Josephus, in no verse of the Septuagint, and in the New Testament at Revelation 3:17 only. (Plato has the phrase before Philo, at Laws 631c as I have it from memory and unchecked; Philo is where it is at home in a Jewish writer.) Now Matthew. The row for this place was the rich one told to sell and have treasure in heaven (19:21). But there is a stretch of the Sermon that no row reached: θησαυρίζετε δὲ ὑμῖν θησαυροὺς ἐν οὐρανῷ, and at once, with no bridge, Ὁ λύχνος τοῦ σώματός ἐστιν ὁ ὀφθαλμός. Treasure, then the eye, sound or bad. In Luke the two sayings are a chapter apart. Read from Laodicea they are one saying: the wealth that sees. Philo supplies what makes Revelation's sentence one thought, and Revelation's sentence is what puts Matthew's two together. And Matthew's next verse returns to wealth: οὐ δύνασθε Θεῷ δουλεύειν καὶ μαμωνᾷ. Wealth, sight, wealth: the eye stands in the middle of the teaching on wealth and is its centre. Luke has the three sayings in three chapters (the eye at 11:34, the treasure at 12:33–34, the two masters at 16:13). Matthew's arrangement is itself the evidence here: the genealogy accounts for why these sayings stand together, which a count of shared words could not.

VI.6b. Philo behind the mark. For Philo the reasoning soul is δόκιμον εἶναι νόμισμα σημειωθὲν καὶ τυπωθὲν σφραγῖδι θεοῦ, ἧς ὁ χαρακτήρ ἐστιν ὁ ἀίδιος λόγος, a true coin struck by the seal of God, whose stamp is the eternal Logos (De plantatione 18); and there are those who strike the true coin false (De mutatione 208). Coin with image, stamp or seal: four sections of Philo's text (De plantatione 18; Legum allegoriae 3.95; De mutatione 208; De virtutibus 4), none of Josephus, no verse of the Septuagint. Revelation has two strikings of persons: the seal of God on the foreheads (7:3) and the mark of the beast, with its image, without which no one buys or sells (13:15–17). A mark on a person that licenses buying is a person made into coin. Matthew's question about the tax then asks whose image the coin bears and gives each striking back to its striker. And Matthew alone names the thing: ἐπιδείξατέ μοι τὸ νόμισμα τοῦ κήνσου, where Mark and Luke say only "a denarius". νόμισμα stands here and nowhere else in the New Testament. It is Philo's word for what God's seal strikes. A reading follows, and it is a reading, with no count behind it. If the coin is Caesar's because it bears Caesar's image, then "what is God's" is what bears God's: the person, the true coin of Philo, the sealed of Revelation. The saying then asks whose striking a person carries, which is Revelation's question at chapter 13 put to a coin.

VI.6c. Two more in the same stretch, both on who is subject to whom. οἱ βασιλεῖς τῆς γῆς … καὶ πᾶς δοῦλος καὶ ἐλεύθερος, the roll of those who hide from the wrath; οἱ βασιλεῖς τῆς γῆς ἀπὸ τίνων λαμβάνουσιν τέλη ἢ κῆνσον, Ἄραγε ἐλεύθεροί εἰσιν οἱ υἱοί. "The kings of the earth" is in the Gospels here only, and with "free" within a verse only here and at Revelation 6:15 and 19:18–19 (written down before counting). The story is Matthew's alone. And ὁ ἄρχων τῶν βασιλέων τῆς γῆς … λύσαντι ἡμᾶς ἐκ τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν ἡμῶν ἐν τῷ αἵματι αὐτοῦ; οἱ ἄρχοντες τῶν ἐθνῶν κατακυριεύουσιν αὐτῶν, δοῦναι τὴν ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ λύτρον ἀντὶ πολλῶν. The title is made a rule of conduct and the loosing its noun, λύω to λύτρον. ἄρχων beside loosing or ransom stands in three places of the New Testament (Revelation 1:5; Matthew 20:25; Luke 24:20–21). The saying is Mark's too; Mark 10:42 has the verb ἄρχειν where Matthew has the noun. Entered for the relation, with one word in common.

VI.7. What kind of economy this makes. The teacher does not leave the market; every term of Revelation's commerce is kept. What changes is what each term is for. What may be bought: the one pearl, where the merchants' pearls go unsold. What counts as wealth: treasure in heaven, by selling, where Laodicea was told to buy. What blood buys: in the Lamb's hand a people, in the priests' hands a graveyard. Who may trade: the beast licenses buyers and sellers, and the teacher clears them out. Whose the coin is: the image is given back to the one it shows. What a day is worth: the rider's price for a measure of wheat is a worker's wage. One question is left over, and it is the one the source itself poses in its last chapter: what is owed to work. The next section is the answer, and it is the end of this one.

## VII. Turn the name into a story

VII.1. This is the one place where the procedure has been run forward and not only read back.

VII.2. The seed is two verses.

> Ἰδοὺ ἔρχομαι ταχύ, καὶ ὁ μισθός μου μετ’ ἐμοῦ, ἀποδοῦναι ἑκάστῳ ὡς τὸ ἔργον ἐστὶν αὐτοῦ.
> ἐγὼ τὸ Ἄλφα καὶ τὸ Ὦ, ὁ πρῶτος καὶ ὁ ἔσχατος, ἡ ἀρχὴ καὶ τὸ τέλος.

VII.3. Under P1 and P2 the speaker cannot say this of himself in a told life. A master in a likeness must do it. Then two things the vision says side by side collide. The wage is to each as his work is. And the first and the last are one. In a vision they never meet. Told as an economy they can be made to collide, and here a line has to be drawn between what the source constrains and what the composer decides. The source gives two sayings. It does not say that "the first and the last" is a statement about workers, or about pay. Reading the title as a claim that the ends are alike in what they receive is a decision, and an inventive one. Once it is taken, the pay cannot differ by the work, and the two sayings contradict. Philo has the terms for it. In the treatise that sets the Logos as the seventh in the midst, the Logos is the divider who alone divides equally, and equality has more than one kind: by number, and ἡ διὰ ἀναλογίας, καθ’ ἣν καὶ τὰ ὀλίγα τοῖς πολλοῖς καὶ τὰ βραχέα τοῖς μείζοσιν ἴσα νενόμισται, by proportion, under which the few are held equal to the many (Quis heres 141–146). "To each as his work is" is equality by proportion. One wage to all is equality by number. Revelation sets the two in consecutive clauses under one speaker who is beginning and end, and "beginning and end" as a pair is in eight sections of Philo, of God at De plantatione 93, and in the New Testament at Revelation 21:6 and 22:13 only. Matthew's first workers name the issue in Philo's word: ἴσους αὐτοὺς ἡμῖν ἐποίησας. This is read, and no count supports it beyond the two phrases. The conserving solution is then to stage the collision (STAGE): workers who differ as much as possible in what they did, the same paid to all, and the paying so ordered that those who did most see it. The title, which no master can bear, becomes the order of the act (ENACT).

VII.4. The rule was written as a pack: the two verses, the two parameters, six forced consequences each with the grammar that enacts it, three searches into the Septuagint. No word of the target was in it. That is a weaker thing than it sounds. The pack was written with the target known, and what it forces may carry the target's shape without the target's words. A key of twelve points on the Greek surface was sealed before any run; one point, the title enacted as the order of the act, counts double, so the score is out of thirteen. Fresh readers, three language models from one maker, each in a separate context, were given the pack and nothing else.

The absence of a source relation is no evidence of another source. Every verse of the Gospel is then one of three things: **recovered** from an antecedent that can be opened; **composed**, made by the act of writing this book; or **unexplained**. A verse with no row is the third until it is shown to be the first or the second, and a lost document is none of the three. A told life marks where a speech ends. That is composition, and of a particular kind: five discourses in one book make five places where a discourse has to end, and the book supplies what its own architecture then requires. A composition generates the conditions of its further composition. It enters the procedure as a rule of C (XIII.1d). Of the residue in the eight chapters read through, about a fifth is such joinery by a crude count; the rest (the bread of 6:11, the herd of 8:30–34, the physician of 9:12, the end of the talents) is written "unexplained", with its address.

The note is not supported by the count and is not refuted by it: what Mark lacks of Josephus's words it mostly lacks by having no scene at all, and distance at the level of who acts was not measured. The order of the Gospels stays a premise in neither direction (0.9).

## XIII. The procedure

XIII.0. The labels. The research record and the ledger name the rules by letter; this paper has mostly used the verbs. P = the three parameters (P1 the speaker given a past; P2 the life told in a named land; P3 the source's own layers open beside it, set out at XIII.1a). M = the moves on who bears a relation: M1 KEEP; M2 EXCHANGE the patient, the adversary's treatment falling on the teacher or on hearers; M3 the costly act of the speaker or the Lamb done by the adversary, or refused; M4 the rider's charge taken by the teacher; M5 OPPOSE commerce, image, wealth; M6 the faithful's trial to the teacher and the seer's part to followers; M7 the speaker's reproofs and exposures said of the adversaries in the land. F = the forms: F1 STAGE, F2 ENACT, F3 LIKEN, F4 FORETELL, F5 HAPPEN. C = composition: the frame; one stretch from many places; one place divided; the furniture let go.

XIII.1. In order.

1. Hold Revelation open. Its unit is the relation, with the bearer it has at that place.
2. **P1.** Give the speaker a past: a teacher in a land, with followers, killed there.
3. **P2.** Make the host a told life in a named land. Name the adversary from the land. Cite as written what is scripture's.
4. **P3.** Keep open, beside each place of the source, what that place was made from: the scripture, and Philo's reading of it. A member the source compressed may be restored. Nothing of Philo's enters except through such a place.
5. Set the frame: the birth and flight of Revelation 12 at the beginning; the death, custody and opening of Revelation 11 and 20, with the first vision's "fear not", at the end.
6. For each stretch of the life, gather every place of the source that holds its relation.
7. Apply to each relation the move its kind requires:

| If the relation in Revelation is | then |
|---|---|
| a first-person saying of the speaker | keep it as the teacher's saying |
| a deed done to the adversary | it is done to the teacher; at the judgment, to hearers who fail |
| a costly act of the speaker or the Lamb toward the faithful | the adversary does it to the teacher, or it is refused |
| a rider's charge, given in the passive | the teacher says it of himself, or it is turned on him |
| commerce, an image, wealth trusted in | the teacher opposes it, keeping its terms |
| the faithful's part | the trial goes to the teacher; the seeing and falling to followers |
| the speaker's reproof of an assembly, or exposure of a false claimant | the teacher says it of the adversaries in the land |

8. Give it a form: stage two colliding sayings as a likeness; enact a title as an act or a list; liken a scene of the end to the kingdom; foretell a sign; or let it happen.
9. Let the vision's furniture go (horses, bowls, trumpets, living creatures, counted thirds) and keep the rare word.

XIII.1a. The third parameter. Revelation was itself made from something: from scripture, and at many places from Philo's reading of scripture (In the Midst of the Seven shows this for the lampstands and the letters; III.7, V.5c, VI.6a–b and VII.3 show it at the places Matthew opens). P3: at each place of the source, the layers it was made from stand open beside it, and a member the source compressed may be restored in the telling. The limit is part of the rule. Philo is admitted only through a place Revelation holds and a row has located. Philo set free against Matthew cannot tell Matthew from Mark (XIV.5b).

The test. Seven predictions were sealed before any search, at the places where In the Midst of the Seven had already fixed Revelation on Philo. Each said: at the row for this place Matthew will show a member of Philo's that Revelation's words do not carry.

| Revelation | Philo's member | Matthew | |

Five of them are three-way: scripture has the words, and what Philo supplies is the join that puts them at this place. That is the structure of II.8 again. And they were predicted by one who knows Philo and Matthew; they are a memory confirmed under seal, with counts, and they are owed a blind run like every other row but the labourers.

XIII.1b. What the third parameter claims, exactly. A member can pass through three texts in three ways. It can be kept at every station. It can be reworked at the middle station and the reworking passed on. Or the middle station can drop it and the last restore it. Only the third is new with P3, and it has a rival: that Matthew had Philo independently and Revelation is not in the line. What tells them apart is a place where Matthew keeps something Revelation made and also has what Revelation dropped.

| Place | Made in Revelation and kept by Matthew | In Philo, not in Revelation's words, and in Matthew |
|---|---|---|
| the sword | λαβεῖν τὴν εἰρήνην ἐκ τῆς γῆς, and a great sword given; βαλεῖν εἰρήνην ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν … ἀλλὰ μάχαιραν. Peace, earth and sword in one verse: these two only | cutting in two: διχάσαι |
| the city | gates of pearl, dogs outside, entering; they shall see | peace beside the seeing; the city as persons |

| the name | a name that is "called" (19:13) | its gloss by saving |

At the wealth that sees, Matthew has only what Revelation has, differently told: that is the second way, and Philo explains it without being restored. In notation, for those who want it: Matthew is T(R; A), where A is what R was made from and is reached only through R. The whole runs from a philosophical relation, through a vision, to a told life with its teaching; and each station does to the material something a reader can name.

XIII.1c. How the word changes, and rows entered for the relation. The first version bound every row by words the two books share. Read back, the rows that hold show five ways a word changes between the books: the root is kept and the form remade (fourteen cases, eight of them a noun or adjective of Revelation become a verb in Matthew, as ἀποκάλυψις, ἀποκαλύψαι; λύω, λύτρον the other way); another thing stands in the same place (τέκνον, παιδίον); the bearer is exchanged; a member Revelation dropped from its own source is restored (XIII.1a); a condition is made a thing. From these a second kind of row was admitted, entered for the relation where few words or none are shared, under a check fixed beforehand: three places or more in the relation; each changed word one of the five kinds; a move the procedure already has; and the configuration rare, six places or fewer in the New Testament, or else the row is marked as read. Seventeen rows are so marked and are counted apart (XIV.1). The control this method needs, the same check run from another book into Matthew, has not been run.

XIII.1d. Since the first reissue: a fourth parameter proposed, the relation one level below the word, and a rule of composition. None of this has been run blind, and T_Μ as tested remains the nine steps above with three parameters.

*A fourth parameter, proposed.* P3 opens what Revelation was made from. Sections E and J open two bodies of text beside it: the letters, and Josephus. **P4, as read: the letters stand open for persons and conduct, and Josephus for circumstance. Where the source gives a power, a sight or a promise and a letter gives a sentence about a named person or about conduct, the first is given to the person of the second and made to happen (E.12). Where a stretch of the life needs an office, a coin, a sect, a place or an incident, it is taken from Josephus, and across chapters 23–27 one passage of the *War* supplies the order (J.5).** Unlike P3 it has no limit yet of the kind that made P3 testable (Philo only through a place Revelation holds), and that limit is what it needs before a pack is written. The division of labour of M.5 is its first description.

*The relation, below the word.* The author: "we need lemmas that can handle transformations - merciful --> giving mercy, even mercy --> cruelty. its the relations that will hold, not the specific lexical form." A module links two passages at four levels: the same lemma; KIN, one derivational family (mercy, merciful, to show mercy, alms, merciless); FIELD, one semantic subdomain; OPP, opposed families (first and last, bind and loose, rich and poor, plague and heal). The tagging is MACULA Greek's on the Nestle 1904 text. Tested on the letters' fifty-two pairs against the same passages shuffled into wrong pairings, with the outcome sealed first:

| Level | Links in the true pairs | In shuffled pairs (mean) | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same lemma | 118 | 5.9 | 20.0 |
| KIN alone | 10 | 2.2 | 4.6 |
| FIELD alone | 4 | 3.2 | 1.3 |
| OPP | 14 | 2.4 | 5.8 |

The family and the opposition tell a true pair from a false one. "Same topic" does not. So the two levels the author named carry weight and the looser one carries none.

Of the ledger's seventeen rows with a turning move, OPP finds opposed words in four. In the other thirteen the words are the same on both sides. The oath is ὀμνύω in both books and the keys are κλείς in both. What turns is who does it, to whom, and whether it is forbidden. The tagging gives each verb a frame, and three rows read off the frames as they stand:

| | Revelation | Matthew | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| The oath | 10:6 ὀμνύω; the doer is the angel | 5:34 ὀμνύω; the doer is the disciple, under μή | the doer; and it is forbidden |
| The keys | 1:18 ἔχω; the speaker has the key | 16:19 δίδωμι; Jesus gives, "you" receive; then δέω, λύω, done by Simon | have becomes give; a receiver is added and then acts |
| The wage | 22:12 ἀποδίδωμι; the speaker pays each | 20:8 ἀποδίδωμι; the steward pays the workers | the giver delegated; "each" becomes a named class |

These are the moves of XIII.1 stated as changes to a frame: EXCHANGE is a change of doer with the verb and its object kept; OPPOSE is the same frame under a negation; STAGE gives the slots to characters. An instrument that names the move from which slot differs is the next thing to build and is not built. The referents in the frames are the tagging editors' reading and are checked row by row; Josephus and Philo have no such tagging.

*A rule of composition, added to C.* **What the book's own architecture requires, the book makes.** Once the life is told in stretches and the teaching gathered into discourses, there are joins to make and speeches to end, and nothing in any source can supply them because no source has that architecture. The first instance: close each discourse with the formula (M.7). The rule gives the grammar its third outcome beside a relation moved and a relation left: material with no antecedent, produced by decisions already taken. Its inventory is owed; a first crude count is in the notebook (4.22.4).

XIII.2. Three of the seven moves are one consequence of P1. The adversary's sufferings, the Lamb's costly acts and the faithful's trial all need somewhere to land once there is a person with a past, and they land on that person.

XIII.3. Why the frame is where it is. The birth and the tomb are the two densest regions of the map, and there is a reason beyond density. A speaker who is and is to come has no beginning and no end. A person with a past has both. P1 cannot be carried out without a birth and a death, and those are the two things a vision of a living speaker has least reason to tell of that speaker. The source tells them of others: a child born in chapter 12, witnesses killed and raised in 11, an adversary shut in and loosed in 20. The frame is what P1 requires, taken from where the source has it.

XIII.4. What the moves change is the kind of thing the material is.

| In Revelation | In Matthew |
|---|---|
| the speaker's utterance | the teacher's saying |
| the assembly's trial | the teacher's experience |
| the seer's encounter | the followers' experience |
| the city's architecture | instruction |
| a rider's charge | something the teacher says of himself or suffers |
| the divine name | the order of a story |
| the adversary's judgment | the teacher's passion |
| a scene of the end | a parable |

What a reader of Matthew sorts into biography, ethics, teaching about the end, parable and passion are, on this procedure, different realizations of material that Revelation holds in one vision. This also bears on the failed tests. A city turned into commands and a custody turned into a burial have no reason to leave the same kind of trace, and a search for one uniform kind of dependence across the book found none.

## XIV. What is not written, and what failed

XIV.1. Reach. The ledger has 158 rows. They reach 305 of Matthew's 1,067 verses, 28.6 per cent; 265 verses, 24.8 per cent, without the seventeen rows entered for relation alone (XIII.1c). The first version of this paper had 86 rows and 178 verses, 16.7 per cent. Every chapter now has a row; chapters 6, 9 and 1 have two. A count made with no reader (pairings that Revelation and Matthew share and no book outside the Gospels has: 287 of them) adds further verses; with it the first draft stood at 21 per cent, and the union has not been recounted for this one. A verse reached is a verse with a relation in it. It is not a verse whose wording the procedure produces.

XIV.1a. Reach by kind of material. All of Matthew is the wrong denominator for a procedure that leaves Mark in place. Matthew's verses were sorted by machine, and the sorting is a proxy and not a synopsis: a verse counts as Mark's if it shares three content words (or half its content words, at least two) with some verse of Mark; failing that, as shared with Luke on the same test; failing that, as Matthew's own. That gives 458, 184 and 425 verses. The ledger reaches 100 of the first (21.8 per cent), 70 of the second (38.0 per cent) and 135 of the third (31.8 per cent): 305 verses. At the first version these stood at 51, 40 and 87. Before any count the order was predicted with Matthew's own material highest; the material shared with Luke has stood first since the rows found through Philo, and stands further ahead now. Two denominators a reviewer asked for are not yet built: Matthew's changes inside passages shared with Mark, and the scenes that carry the book's structure. Both need a synopsis in the seat.

XIV.2. Unreached. Several chapters have two or three rows. Of the eighty verses of chapters 11 and 12, twenty are reached and the rest are held out of the search, so that a frozen grammar can be tried on them. The genealogy, the body of the Sermon, the healings, the sabbath controversies and most of the narrative shared with Mark are outside. The procedure says "for each stretch of the life" and does not say what the stretches are or their order, beyond the frame. The narrative Matthew shares with Mark is outside it. Whatever account is given of Matthew, Mark is in it.

XIV.3. Unexplained within reach. Where a relation fits two conditions, the procedure does not say which governs. The seat is kept at 19:28 and refused at 20:23. A reviewer asks whether the circumstances of the asking decide it, and the source suggests they do. The promise has a condition in it: Ὁ νικῶν δώσω αὐτῷ καθίσαι μετ’ ἐμοῦ, to the one who conquers, ὡς κἀγὼ ἐνίκησα καὶ ἐκάθισα μετὰ τοῦ Πατρός μου. At 19:28 the seats are told to those who have left everything, as what they shall have. At 20:22 the seat is asked for as rank, and the answer is the condition put as a question, δύνασθε πιεῖν τὸ ποτήριον ὃ ἐγὼ μέλλω πίνειν, and then the Father of the promise's second half: it is for those for whom it is prepared ὑπὸ τοῦ Πατρός μου. So nothing is refused that the source gave. The source's condition is staged. As a rule: a promise with a condition is kept where the condition is taken as met, and its condition is made a trial where the promise is claimed. That is one case, and a rule from one case is a proposal.

XIV.4. Written down before counting, for the first version of this paper. Eighteen lines for the land, the childhood, the teaching and the adversaries. Thirteen held as written. Three failed as written: the children killed (placed at Matthew 2, found at 23:37); blood poured out on the earth (the Revelation verse and an Acts verse were not as stated); Satan cast out (said to be absent from Luke, and it is there). One was too loose to fail and one could not be counted. Ten of the eighteen were expected to be Matthew's alone among the Gospels; seven are, and three are shared with John or Luke. The prediction on the miracles failed (XII.2). For the third draft, eight more: three held (seeing God; mourning ended, with Isaiah nearer; reach highest in Matthew's own material), one failed (the Sermon was predicted to share more of the vocabulary of Revelation 21–22 than four other stretches of Matthew, and ranks fourth of the five), one was predicted to be common stock and is, and three were expectations about the Septuagint, each confirmed against the rows of II.5 and III.1.

XIV.4a. Written down before counting, since the first version. For rows: sixteen lines on the residue, nine held and six failed in whole or part; nineteen on rows entered for relation, thirteen held outright and one failed outright; seven on the passion and Peter, five held (Peter was predicted reached in 60 per cent of the verses that name him and stands at 12 of 23); ten on the held-out chapters from the author's reading, five held (I.5a); five on a reviewer's four predictions, of which two gave a row and one of those weak. For counts on scripture (S.7): of nine lines, five held and one in part, and of the three on the reverse direction two went against. Every line, with the hour it was written and its result, is in a file kept off every public surface so that it cannot be read by a model later asked to score blind; the notebook gives the results.

XIV.5a. Philo behind Revelation, at the places Matthew opens. Nine places of Revelation, each located by a row, with what Philo was expected to hold, were written down before Philo was searched. Found as written: the city as Jerusalem, vision of peace, in the soul (V.5c); blind wealth (VI.6a); the twelve stars as Philo's count (III.7); the soul as coin under God's seal (VI.6b); beginning and end, and the two equalities, the second more exact than expected (VII.3); the mother of the Logos (III.7), the weakest join; the two tiers of oath (V.3a). Failed: Revelation's "I reprove and discipline" was expected to join Philo's form of the proverb to the Septuagint's; the Septuagint as seated already has Philo's form, and Philo is not needed. Half: "called spiritually Sodom and Egypt". Philo has Egypt as the body in 39 sections and Sodom as blindness and barrenness in two, and never the two names together. Seven of nine, then, with one failure and one half. Clustering, which was the criterion that carried the seven lampstands: three of the places (the twelve stars, Sodom, the city) stand in one book, De somniis 2; and the treatise of the seventh in the midst, Quis heres, also holds the divider's equality and blind wealth. What this is worth: the places were chosen by one who knows Philo, so a found place confirms a memory. The counts beside each (Josephus, the Septuagint, the rest of the New Testament) are what a reader can check.

XIV.5c. What P3 has to earn. A reviewer sets four procedures side by side: scripture and the received sources alone; those with Revelation; those with Philo free; those with Philo admitted through Revelation. The third has been run and cannot tell Matthew from Mark (XIV.5b). The fourth has now been run in small at one place, with a control (VII.10). The full comparison, at the wealth and the eye, the oath and the two blessings, each as a pack under each procedure, is owed.

XIV.5b. Philo straight to Matthew: a wrong edge, run first. The author's instruction was Philo to Revelation; the first test ran Philo to Matthew, and it is kept as run. An instrument was declared and sealed before it was built: pairs of rare words in a verse of Matthew that stand together in one section of Philo and in no verse of the Septuagint, with Josephus as the control. Three predictions. That Philo's excess over Josephus would be greater in Matthew's own and shared-with-Luke material than in its Markan material: failed (37 and 29 per cent against 35). That Mark would not show the same excess: failed; Mark shows it (ratio 1.30, against 1.22 for Matthew and 1.49 for Luke). By the terms of the seal that empties the rest: the instrument measures how far Philo's Greek overlaps any Gospel's, and says nothing of Matthew. Five places were also named from memory. Found as named: the oath (V.3a); "the one who sees God", Philo's standing gloss of Israel, in 54 sections and in no verse of the Septuagint, beside Matthew 5:8; εἰρηνοποιός, said of God once in Philo (Spec. 2.192), beside "the peacemakers shall be called sons of God", the noun's one place in the New Testament. Not as named: treasure with heaven is the Septuagint's (Deut 28:12); desire with adultery is in Philo six times and is a looser thing than Matthew 5:28. The oath, and the two blessings, were found on this edge and are better explained on the other (V.3a, V.5c).

XIV.5. Failed earlier, under predictions sealed beforehand. Revelation's order as Matthew's order: three ways (by section, by rotation at the centre, by runs in either direction). One mapping of seats over the whole book: the seats kept; the three seats that live in "I" and "you"; a general extraction of every relation, five predictions of five. A law for each Gospel by the frequency of its operations. The assignment of the four riders' strands to the four Gospels as first counted (it came within chance, and came out as Irenaeus has it only under a rule made afterwards). These are in the research record with their numbers.

XIV.5d. Order, tried again at 149 and 158 rows, each test fixed before it was run. Matthew cut into blocks against the parts of Revelation: p = 0.54. Whether neighbours in Matthew are neighbours in Revelation: ratio 0.92, p = 0.135. The longest chain rising or falling: 27 and 25, p = 0.17 and 0.50. Rank correlation over all rows: −0.09, p = 0.13, backward in sign and not established; it is −0.42 among rows the author's side hypothesized and +0.06 among rows read or counted, which is a warning about the first figure. Any rotation of Revelation: p = 0.76. Rows of one strand, by the ledger's own labels: 47 per cent in the same order, p = 0.73. Rows of one letter or chapter: 358 pairs to 365, with the single exception of II.6a. Unmapped verses lying between two neighbouring rows do not share more with what lies between in Revelation: the order predicts nothing by interpolation. Matthew's own scripture gives the reason no order should be expected: thirteen uses of Isaiah at −0.14, fourteen of the Psalms at +0.01 (0.6).

XIV.5e. The scripture counts (S.7) rest on three lists made from memory: Matthew's open citations, Matthew's tacit uses, Revelation's uses. Each locus was checked by machine in the seated Septuagint, and the instrument is crude enough to lose Hosea 11:1 at Matthew 2:15, where Matthew's Greek is not the Septuagint's. A published index of allusions and a lemmatized Septuagint are owed before those counts are more than first ones. The recoding of what a turning does (S.3) was done by one reader who knew the thesis.

XIV.6. Other accounts. The two-source hypothesis, the Farrer–Goulder hypothesis and the two-Gospel hypothesis each say how Matthew stands to Mark and Luke. None places Revelation among Matthew's sources, and this paper does not displace any of them on the ground they cover. It adds a relation they do not consider and shows it is workable as a procedure. Sim (1996) shows that Matthew holds the outlook of apocalyptic eschatology throughout, and compares it with Revelation among others; that is a shared outlook and makes no claim of source. Goulder is the nearest in kind: a writer with one source, expanding it by stated habits of composition. His source was Mark and his habits were midrash and the lectionary. The procedure here has a different source and reaches different parts of the book. Whether the two can be run together on one text, and what each then has left to do, has not been tried and is a question of substance.

XIV.7. Direction. The rows are relations between two texts. Section VII shows that a stated rule produces Matthew's parable from Revelation's two verses: the two verses suffice under the rule supplied. Two cuts were tried, the title alone and the wage alone (Appendix A.2, A.3), and neither produced it. That is sufficiency under one rule and the failure of two cuts. It is no demonstration that both verses are necessary under every rule. No row shows that the rule was run in the first century, or in which direction. One argument from the rows themselves has been put and counted. Read from Revelation to Matthew the operations are those Matthew performs on scripture (S). Read the other way they are different operations: a denial written as an assembly's praise, a guiding star as stars thrown down. If Revelation never treats a source so, the reverse reading asks this writer to break its practice for one book. The count did not bear that out. Revelation's own scripture, on the same instrument, shares a median of two stems a row, as Matthew's does, and the rows between the two books stand as far from the one practice as from the other; and the letters do make scripture's stories into promise and charge (the manna, Balaam, Jezebel). The argument stands as a reading of particular rows. What decides direction, on the programme's account, is not in this paper: the number and the mechanisms of the borrowings taken together across the epistles and the Gospels, and the genealogy of P.2, which runs through extant texts in one order only.

XIV.8. Since the first reissue: what failed, what is owed, and three corrections of method.

*Instruments that failed.* Five automatic finders were built to locate the letters, Philo or Josephus in Matthew without a reader, each sealed before its run, and all five failed: two rules of rare shared lemmas over the ledger, a third built to price chance, a pair count against Philo, and a finder at the level of the word family whose own null was faulty. What works in this paper is a reader naming pairs from memory, a sealed list, and a check of the words. That is a limit of the method and is stated as one.

*Controls.* The first reissue's counts on Philo used Josephus as the control. At the author's ruling a control must stand outside the hypothesized system, and Josephus and Acts are withdrawn as controls. Run again against Greek prose assumed outside it, the share of Matthew's verses holding a rare pair with each author is: Philo 6.0 per cent, Epictetus 9.3, Diodorus 7.5, Strabo 5.3, Maximus of Tyre 3.9. Philo is not distinguished by that instrument, which confirms XIV.5b from a second side.

*The reader.* Every list in sections E, J, T and M was made by one reader in one sitting each. The exchange test of M.3 measures the rule. At *how to write Matthew from Revelation*:

"How to Write Matthew from Revelation" is a paper by Lee Sharks (*Grammata: Journal of Operative Philology*, Crimson Hexagonal Archive, 2026) in the Revelation First programme. It asks what a writer holding Revelation would have to do to get a Gospel, and answers by doing it: twenty-one rules in five groups (the source held whole; a past, a land and the layers behind the source; keep, exchange and reassign; stage, enact, liken, foretell, happen; five checks) applied to named places of Revelation, each result set beside Matthew's Greek.
